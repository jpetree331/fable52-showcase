Alembic

A chemistry workbench, distilled from first principles.

Balance an equation

Write a reaction, with or without coefficients. Charges go after a caret — Fe^3+, SO4^2- — and an electron is e-. Pasted superscripts such as Fe³⁺ work too.

How it works

Each species gets an unknown coefficient, and each element — plus net charge, when ions are present — gets a conservation equation. The balanced equations are exactly the null space of that matrix. It is found by Gaussian elimination over exact fractions (arbitrary-precision integers, never floating point), then scaled to the smallest whole numbers.

The size of the null space is the honest part. One dimension means one answer. Zero means the equation cannot balance, and the element at fault is named. Two or more means the "equation" is several independent reactions written as one, and no amount of algebra can choose between them — so the page says so rather than guessing.

Draw a molecule

Enter a SMILES string: atoms as letters, = and # for double and triple bonds, parentheses for branches, matching digits to close rings, lowercase for aromatic atoms.

How it works

A hand-written parser turns the string into a graph, fills in the hydrogens each atom's valence implies, and finds the rings with Horton's minimum-cycle-basis algorithm. Aromatic rings are then kekulized: a perfect-matching search assigns the alternating double bonds, and a ring with no valid assignment is rejected rather than drawn.

Coordinates come from geometry, not from a template library. Rings are placed as arcs of a circle chosen so every bond has the same length — one rule that produces fused hexagons, five-membered rings and spiro centres alike — and chains grow outward into whatever angle is free. Cage molecules that cannot lie flat fall back to a spring-relaxed projection. Stereochemistry is read but not drawn.

Run a titration

Choose what is in the flask and what is in the burette. Move across the curve to read the pH at any volume.

How it works

There are no special cases here — no separate formula for a weak acid, a buffer, or the far side of the equivalence point. At every volume the page writes down one equation, the charge balance, and solves it for [H⁺] by bisection, which cannot fail to converge because the imbalance falls steadily as pH rises.

Henderson–Hasselbalch and the other textbook shortcuts are approximations of that one equation; the test suite checks that they agree with it wherever they are supposed to. The model assumes 25 °C and an ideal solution, so a real pH meter will drift from it in concentrated or strongly ionic solutions.
